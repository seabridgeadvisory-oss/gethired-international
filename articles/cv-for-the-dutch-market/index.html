Reading your CV like a Dutch recruiter

Your CV is not wrong. It’s written for a different market.

The CV that worked back home is quietly working against you here. What a Dutch recruiter is scanning for, and the handful of changes that fix most of it.

Most internationals arrive with a CV that served them perfectly well at home, and that is exactly the problem. A Dutch recruiter is scanning for different signals, in a different length, in a different tone, and none of that has anything to do with how good you actually are at your job. The document is not wrong. It is addressed to the wrong reader.

What follows is what changes when you re-address it — the structural conventions, the tone, and the handful of lines that quietly decide whether anyone reads page two.

Two pages, not one

If you were taught that a CV must fit on one page, let that go. Two pages is standard here, and cramming a decade of work onto one page reads as thin, not sharp. The one-page rule comes from markets where recruiters screen enormous volumes of near-identical graduate applications. That is not what is happening when a Dutch hiring manager opens your file.

Use the space for what it is actually for:

  • The last three roles in real detail. Four to six lines each, and those lines should say what changed, not what the job description said.
  • Earlier roles compressed to a line or two. Title, employer, dates, and one clause if something there genuinely matters.
  • Anything more than about fifteen years back as a single grouped line. “Earlier roles in retail operations, 2006–2011 — details on request” is a perfectly respectable way to end a career history.
  • Nothing padded to fill a gap. A second page that is half empty is worse than a tight single page, so let the content decide the length rather than the other way round.

Reverse chronological order is expected. The skills-based or functional CV, where roles are hidden behind competency headings, is read here as an attempt to disguise something — usually a gap or a career change. If you have either, it is better to name it in one honest line than to restructure the whole document around hiding it. A sentence like “2023: relocated to the Netherlands; six months on language study and settling the family” closes the question. An unexplained hole invites a worse guess than the truth.

The header block decides more than you think

The top of page one is read first, read fastest, and read by everyone — the software, the screener and the hiring manager. It should contain exactly what a reader needs to place you, and nothing decorative.

  • Name, city, phone, email, LinkedIn. A Dutch mobile number and a Dutch city, if you have them, remove a doubt before it forms.
  • Your work authorization, in one line. See below — this is the single most-skipped line on an international CV.
  • Languages with honest levels. Not buried in a sidebar on page two. If Dutch is relevant to the role, the reader wants it now.
  • A three- or four-line profile. Who you are professionally, what you have done, what you are looking for. Specifics, not adjectives.

Nothing important should sit in the document’s actual header or footer region, by the way. Parsing software frequently skips those entirely, and a phone number nobody can extract is a phone number nobody rings.

The line most people forget

The line that quietly kills the most applications is work authorization. A recruiter looking at a foreign name and a foreign address will not spend a phone call finding out whether hiring you means a permit process. They will move on, and you will never know that was the reason.

Example

If you already have the right to work: “Dutch work authorization: EU national, no permit or sponsorship required.”

If you would need sponsorship: “Eligible for the highly skilled migrant scheme; relocating to the Netherlands from March.”

If you hold a partner or dependent permit: “Residence permit as partner of a Dutch resident; free access to the labour market, no sponsorship required.” Almost nobody states this, and it removes the employer’s entire objection.

One line, in your header block, next to your location. You are answering the question before it becomes a reason to stop reading.

Say what changed, not what you were responsible for

Dutch business writing is understated, almost blunt. Calling yourself a results-driven visionary will not read as confident; it will read as noise a busy recruiter skims straight past. Say what you did, say what changed because of it, and let the specifics do the persuading.

The pattern that works is unglamorous: what you did → what changed → how you know. Most CVs stop at the first part.

Example — logistics

Before: “Responsible for managing the regional logistics budget and a team of eight, driving operational excellence across the network.”

After: “Led eight people across regional logistics. Consolidated three carrier contracts into one, which took roughly 12% out of outbound freight cost over 18 months.”

Same job. The second version tells a reader what you would do for them, and gives them something to ask about in an interview.

Example — when there is no number

Before: “Responsible for stakeholder engagement and change management during a system implementation.”

After: “Ran the business side of an ERP rollout across four sites. Two had rejected the previous attempt; both went live on schedule the second time, with the site managers presenting the plan rather than me.”

No percentage anywhere, and it is still concrete. What changed is the thing to name — a process that stopped breaking, a backlog that cleared, an audit that passed, a team that stopped losing people.

Inventing a percentage is worse than not having one, because the first competent interviewer will ask how you measured it, and a number you cannot defend undoes everything else on the page. If you genuinely do not have one, plain language is not a fallback — it is often more credible.

Translating your career, not just your words

This is the part almost nobody does, and it costs more than any formatting choice. Your reader has thirty seconds and no context for the institutions on your CV. Everything they have to guess at is a risk they do not have to take.

  • Job titles. Internal titles rarely travel. If your title was Specialist II or Officer Grade 5, add a short clarification: “Specialist II (equivalent to Procurement Manager; owned a €12m category).” A screener who cannot place your level will place you at the bottom of it.
  • Employers. “Senior Analyst, Grupo Teixeira” means nothing here. “Senior Analyst, Grupo Teixeira (Brazilian food manufacturer, ~4,000 staff)” costs you six words and places you instantly. One parenthesis per employer is enough.
  • Education. Degree names and grading systems do not map cleanly. Say what the qualification is equivalent to in plain terms, and if the role is one where credentials matter — engineering, healthcare, education, law — a formal credential evaluation from IDW or Nuffic is worth having in hand before anyone asks.
  • Scale and currency. Convert budgets and revenues to euros, or at least give an order of magnitude. A reader who has to look up an exchange rate will simply not.

None of this is inflation. You are supplying the context a local candidate would never have to supply, because for them it is assumed.

The conventions nobody mentions

A few things differ from what you may be used to, and they are easy to get right once you know:

  • Personal details are normal here. Name, city, phone, email, LinkedIn. Date of birth and nationality are commonly included and are not the taboo they are in the US or UK. Include them or do not — their presence will not count against you.
  • A photo is optional and common. If you use one, use a plain, well-lit, professional photo. A holiday crop will do more harm than no photo at all.
  • Dates in European format. Write months as words where there is any room for doubt: 03/04/2026 is read as 3 April here, not 4 March.
  • Skip “references available on request.” It takes a line and tells the reader nothing. They will ask when they want them.
  • Languages matter, so be precise. List them with honest levels, ideally on the CEFR scale. “Dutch (A2, in class twice a week)” is respected. “Dutch (basic)” is ambiguous, and ambiguity gets read pessimistically.
  • Keep the layout plain. One column, standard headings, no text boxes or tables. Beyond looking calmer, it survives the software that may read it before a person does.
  • Hobbies are optional and genuinely read. Dutch CVs often carry two or three. Keep them specific and true — they occasionally start the conversation that gets you remembered.

The cover letter is not dead here

In several markets the cover letter has quietly become a formality. In the Netherlands the cover letter is still read, particularly by smaller employers and in the public sector, and for an international candidate it is the only place you can explain the things a CV cannot.

Keep it to one page and roughly four paragraphs: why this role at this company specifically, the one or two pieces of your experience that map onto their actual problem, your situation in the Netherlands stated plainly, and a direct closing line. Address a person by name where you can find one — hiring teams here are small and usually findable. And write it in the same understated register as the CV; a letter that oversells undoes a CV that did not.

If the vacancy is written in Dutch, the language of your letter becomes a decision in itself, and it is worth thinking about separately.

Your LinkedIn is part of the application

Assume your profile is opened within a minute of your CV, because it usually is. Three things matter more than the rest: the headline, the location, and whether the two documents tell the same story.

Set your location to the Dutch city you are targeting if you have moved or are moving — many recruiters filter by it, and a profile still showing a city abroad is invisible to that search. Make the headline say what you do, not what you want: “Supply chain planner — S&OP, demand planning — Rotterdam” works harder than “Seeking new opportunities.” And make sure dates and titles match your CV exactly. A discrepancy that is really a typo still reads, to a screener, as something to check.

Before you send it

  • Can a stranger tell, in ten seconds, what you do and what level you work at?
  • Is your work authorization status visible without scrolling?
  • Does every bullet say what changed, not just what you oversaw?
  • Would a Dutch reader be able to place every employer and qualification without searching for it?
  • Have you cut every word that would sound inflated read aloud in a quiet room?
  • Can you select the text in your own PDF?
  • Is the file named something a recruiter can find later — Surname-Firstname-CV.pdf, not CV-final-v3.pdf?

None of this is about pretending to be someone you are not. It is about being read correctly by someone with thirty seconds and a stack of a hundred CVs.
