Why applications go unanswered

200 applications, zero replies. The problem is rarely you.

Sending more will not fix it. An application is judged at four separate gates, and silence looks identical at every one — so the first job is working out which one stopped you.

When nothing comes back, the instinct is to send more. It almost never works, because volume was never the constraint. Two hundred applications that all fail for the same reason are not two hundred attempts — they are one attempt, repeated. The useful question is not “how do I send more?” but “which of the four gates am I failing at, and why?”

That is the whole of this piece. Every application passes through four checkpoints, and silence looks identical whichever one stopped you. Separating them is most of the work.

The four gates, and why silence tells you nothing

Between you pressing send and a hiring manager deciding to talk to you, your application is assessed four times, by four different readers with four different jobs:

  • The software. An applicant tracking system parses your file into fields. It is not scoring your potential; it is trying to read your document.
  • The recruiter or HR screener. Usually not an expert in your discipline. They are matching your CV against a vacancy text, fast, and sorting into yes / no / maybe.
  • The hiring manager. The only reader who understands your actual work. They see a shortlist, not the pile.
  • The practical check. Work authorization, notice period, location, salary band. Any of these can end it without anyone reading a word of your experience.

Here is the problem: a rejection at gate one and a rejection at gate four produce exactly the same email, or more often no email at all. So people rewrite their bullet points — a gate-three fix — when they were actually being stopped at gate four. Months go by. Nothing changes, because the thing that was broken was never touched.

Before you rewrite anything, work out which gate you are losing at. The rest of this article is organised that way.

Gate four first: the cheapest problem to fix

Start at the end, because the practical check is the most common silent killer for internationals and the easiest thing to repair.

A recruiter looking at a foreign name, a foreign phone number and an address abroad has one question they will not call you to ask: does hiring this person involve a permit? Most will not find out. They will move to the next file, and you will never know that was the reason.

One line, near the top

If you can already work here: “Dutch work authorization: EU national, no permit or sponsorship required.”

If you would need sponsorship: “Eligible for the highly skilled migrant scheme; available from March, relocating at my own cost.”

If you are a partner of someone with a permit: say so explicitly — a dependent residence permit with free access to the labour market removes the employer’s problem entirely, and almost nobody mentions it.

You are answering the question before it becomes a reason to stop reading. It belongs in your header block, next to your location, not on page two.

The same logic applies to the other practical filters. If your address is still abroad, say when you are available and that the move is already decided. If the posting names a salary range and you are far outside it, you are not being ignored — you are being filed. And if a role is in Groningen and your CV says Lisbon, one line about why the Netherlands and why now does more than another paragraph of achievements.

Gate one: the silent filter

If a company runs your CV through software before a human ever sees it, you are not imagining that. Applicant tracking systems are standard practice at any employer hiring at volume. You cannot game them reliably, and anyone selling you a trick is overstating it. What you can do is stop being filtered for reasons that have nothing to do with your suitability:

  • Use the vacancy’s own job title somewhere in your CV, if it is honestly true of you. If your title was Specialist II and this market calls the role Procurement Manager, a line of clarification stops a screener filing you at the wrong level — and stops the parser missing you entirely.
  • One column. No tables, text boxes or sidebars. They are read out of order or dropped. The two-column template that looks sharp on your screen can arrive as a scramble.
  • Nothing important in a header or footer. Those regions are frequently skipped. Putting your phone number and email there is the classic own goal.
  • Ordinary section names — Experience, Education, Skills. Clever headings confuse the parser and save you nothing.
  • A text-based PDF, not a scan or an image. If you cannot select the text in your own file, neither can the software. Open your PDF and try to highlight a sentence; it takes five seconds and it is the single highest-value check on this list.
  • Spell things out once. “S&OP (Sales and Operations Planning)” matches both the acronym and the phrase. A search for one will not find the other.

One more thing worth knowing: many systems deduplicate by email address. If you applied to the same company three times this year with slightly different documents, the system may be showing a recruiter your oldest file. Applying again to a company that has already said no rarely helps; a message to a human there sometimes does.

Gate two: your first reader has thirty seconds

Your first human reader is usually not an expert in your field. They are matching your CV against a vacancy text, not evaluating your career, so anything that requires guessing what you mean is a risk you cannot afford.

Two practical consequences. First, the top third of page one does almost all the work. Everything you most want read belongs there — not in a career summary at the bottom, not implied by your job titles. Second, lead with what changed, not what you were responsible for. “Responsible for the regional logistics budget” tells a screener your job description. “Consolidated three carrier contracts into one, which took roughly 12% out of outbound freight cost” tells them what you would do for them.

There is a Dutch dimension to this that trips up people arriving from markets where confident self-promotion is expected. Dutch business writing is understated to the point of bluntness. A CV full of visionary, results-driven and passionate does not read as confident here; it reads as noise, and a busy reader skims straight past it to find the facts. Cut the adjectives and the facts have room to land.

Gate three: a vacancy is a problem statement

This is the gate where real rewriting pays, and it is the one most people never reach because they are stuck at the others.

A vacancy exists because something is broken: a team is behind, someone left, a market needs opening, a system needs replacing. The requirements list is a rough attempt by somebody in HR to describe who could fix that. Read it a second time and ask what breaks if the role stays empty for six months. Then build your application around being the answer to that, rather than around ticking bullet points.

Example — supply chain

The vacancy says: “5+ years in supply chain. Experience with S&OP. Strong stakeholder management. Fluent English.”

What it probably means: their forecasting and their production planning disagree with each other, nobody owns the meeting where that gets resolved, and the last person who tried could not get commercial and operations in the same room.

So the opening line becomes: “I have run S&OP cycles in two businesses where commercial and operations were working from different numbers. In both cases the fix was less about the forecast model and more about who sat in the room.”

That is the same experience either way. One version matches bullets; the other answers the question the hiring manager actually has.

Example — a quieter one

The vacancy says: “Finance Business Partner. Period-end close, budgeting cycle, business partnering with commercial teams. Experience in a fast-growing environment.”

What it probably means: the company grew faster than its finance function, the month-end close is late and manual, and the commercial team has stopped trusting the numbers it is given.

So the opening line becomes: “I have twice taken a month-end close from a scramble to a routine — once by cutting the close from eleven days to five, mostly by removing reconciliations nobody was using any more.”

Notice that neither example claims anything the candidate did not do. The work is in choosing which true thing to lead with.

You can usually find the real problem without inside information. Look at how long the role has been open and whether it has been reposted. Check whether they are hiring two of them, or whether the same team has three vacancies — that is a team being rebuilt, not a team being topped up. Read the company’s last six months of announcements: a new market, a funding round, a merger, a regulator’s decision. And look at who held the role before, if LinkedIn will tell you, and where they went.

The channel beats the rewrite

Here is the part most people miss entirely, and it is the one I would change first if I could only change one thing. The channel matters more than another rewrite.

A cold application through a job portal competes with hundreds of near-identical submissions and arrives with no context. The same application sent through a referral, or through a recruiter who works your niche, is a completely different conversation — and it is not a coincidence that referrals consistently outperform job board applications. Specialist recruiters for internationals, like Undutchables, exist for exactly this reason.

“Get a referral” is easy advice and vague help, so here is the version that works when you do not know anyone yet:

  • Find two or three people doing your job at the company — not the recruiter, not the hiring manager. A peer has no gatekeeping role and no reason to be defensive.
  • Ask them about the work, not for a job. A short, specific question about how the team is set up gets answered far more often than a request for help. “Does your team own the data model as well as the pipelines, or is that split?” is a question someone enjoys answering.
  • Mention that you are applying, once, after they reply. Many Dutch companies pay a referral bonus, so an internal nudge costs them nothing and may earn them something.
  • Give it a week before the application, not an hour. A referral that arrives after your application has already been rejected is wasted.

This is slower per role. That is the point: it only works because you are doing it for a handful of roles rather than two hundred.

Two Dutch-market specifics are worth knowing here. The unsolicited application — approaching a company that has no advertised vacancy — is a normal, accepted thing here in a way it is not everywhere, particularly at smaller firms. And directness is not rudeness: a short, plain message saying who you are, what you do and what you are asking for will be received better than a carefully hedged one. Dutch professional culture rewards getting to the point.

Diagnosing your own silence

With the four gates in mind, your own history becomes readable. Work through what has actually happened:

  • No acknowledgement at all, ever, anywhere. Suspect gate one or gate four. Check that your PDF’s text is selectable, that nothing important sits in the header, and that your work authorization is stated on page one.
  • Automatic acknowledgements, then nothing. Gate two. Your document is being read and is not making its case in thirty seconds. Fix the top third of page one.
  • Rejections that arrive quickly, by email, from a person. Also gate two, but someone looked. Often a level or specialism mismatch — you may be applying slightly above or below where your experience reads.
  • First conversations that go well, then silence. Gate three or a practical check surfacing late. Worth asking directly; at this stage people often answer.
  • Interviews that reach the final round and stop. Not an application problem at all. That is an interview and negotiation problem, and rewriting your CV will not touch it.

None of this is possible without a record. If you are not tracking what you sent, through which channel, and what came back, you cannot tell which change worked — and you will end up changing everything at once and learning nothing. A spreadsheet with six columns is enough.

If you reset this week

The honest version of a reset is smaller than most people expect:

  • Stop applying for a few days. Nothing is being lost; the current approach is returning nothing.
  • Run the gate-one checks. Twenty minutes, once, on the file you have been sending for months.
  • Put your work authorization on page one. Five minutes. Possibly the highest-return five minutes of the whole search.
  • Pick twenty roles you would genuinely take, not two hundred you could do.
  • For five of them, find the real problem behind the vacancy and rewrite your opening lines around it.
  • For those same five, find one human being at each company before you submit.
  • Track it. Date, company, channel, who you spoke to, what came back.

Then give it three weeks before you judge the result. Hiring here moves at its own pace: holidays empty an office completely, a hiring manager on leave can stall a process for a fortnight, and a notice period of one to three months is normal on both sides. A delay is not always a rejection, and a rejection is not always final — roles get re-opened, and the second-choice candidate gets a call more often than you would think.

Silence is not a verdict on you. It is almost always a matching failure, and matching failures are fixable — but only once you know which one you have.
