What internationals should know

The 30% ruling isn’t automatic. Here’s what actually qualifies you.

A real tax benefit with real conditions attached — a shrinking percentage, a threshold tested on the wrong number, and a deadline that has already passed for some people.

The 30% ruling is a tax benefit, not a bonus you are handed for being foreign. It lets your employer pay a portion of your salary free of payroll tax instead of the full taxed amount, in recognition of the real costs of relocating for work. Over several years that is genuine money — which is exactly why it is worth understanding before you sign an offer, rather than after.

It is also the part of a Dutch offer that candidates most often misread, because the headline number everyone remembers is no longer the number that applies, and because the thing it is tested against is not the salary written in your contract.

How it actually works

The mechanism is simpler than the arithmetic around it. Your employer designates part of your gross salary as a tax-free allowance for the costs of coming here. Only the remainder is taxed as income. Your gross does not go up; the taxed portion of it goes down, and your net pay rises as a result.

Two consequences follow from that, and both matter. First, the benefit belongs to the employment relationship, not to you personally — it is granted to employer and employee jointly, and it does not survive a job change on its own. Second, because your taxable salary is lower, so are the things calculated from it: pension accrual in many schemes, and benefits based on taxable income such as unemployment or disability payments. That is not a reason to decline it, but it is a reason to read your pension terms rather than assume.

What actually qualifies you

It is not automatic. The conditions are specific, and all of them have to hold:

  • Recruited from abroad. You were hired from another country or transferred in within a multinational. Already living here and then finding a job generally does not qualify.
  • The 150 kilometre rule. In the 24 months before your first working day, you must have lived more than 150 km from the Dutch border for more than 16 of them. This quietly excludes most people moving from Belgium, Luxembourg and large parts of western Germany — and it catches people who did a master’s degree just over the border.
  • A Dutch withholding agent. Your employer has to be liable for Dutch payroll tax. A foreign employer with no Dutch payroll presence cannot grant it.
  • Specific expertise, measured as salary. In practice “scarce expertise” is tested through a minimum taxable salary, indexed every year. Scientific researchers at designated institutions and doctors training as specialists are exempt from the salary test.
  • A decision from the tax authorities. You and your employer apply jointly and receive a formal decision. Until that arrives, nothing is in place.

The threshold applies after the deduction, not before

This is the detail that catches the most people, and it is worth slowing down for. The minimum salary test is applied to your taxable salary — what is left once the tax-free portion has been carved out — not to the gross figure in your contract.

Worked example

Take a gross salary of €65,000 in 2026, with the ruling at 30%.

The tax-free part would be €19,500, leaving a taxable salary of €45,500. That is below the 2026 general threshold of €48,013 — so the full 30% cannot be applied.

What happens in practice is that the allowance is reduced rather than refused: the employer applies a smaller tax-free percentage, just enough to keep taxable salary at the threshold. Here that leaves roughly €17,000 tax-free instead of €19,500.

So a salary that looks comfortably above the norm can fall below it once the deduction is taken, and the benefit shrinks accordingly. If you are negotiating, this is the arithmetic to do before you agree a number — not after payroll explains it to you in February.

The numbers, year by year

The thresholds are indexed annually, so the year your ruling runs in is the year that counts. As published by the Dutch government and the tax authorities:

  • 2024 — €46,107 general; €35,048 if you are under 30 with a qualifying master’s degree.
  • 2025 — €46,660 general; €35,468 reduced.
  • 2026 — €48,013 general; €36,497 reduced.
  • 2027 — legislated at €50,436 general and €38,388 reduced, before the usual indexation is applied, so the final figures may be higher.

Three things about that reduced threshold are easy to miss. It applies only while you are under 30 and only with a qualifying master’s degree. It runs up to and including the month you turn 30 — after which the general threshold applies, which can mean your allowance shrinks mid-career for no reason connected to your performance. And the test is re-applied every year, so a year of part-time work, unpaid leave or a reduced contract can take you under it.

At the other end, the benefit is capped. The salary eligible for the deduction is limited to the ceiling set by the Standards for Remuneration Act, which for 2026 is €262,000. Above that, the untaxed portion stops growing.

What changed, and what changes on 1 January 2027

The terms have been revised repeatedly in recent years, and a lot of advice online is describing a version of the rules that no longer exists. The current position:

  • The percentage drops from 30% to 27% on 1 January 2027. This is not limited to new arrivals. It applies to rulings that started on or after 1 January 2024 as well, for the remainder of their term.
  • An earlier plan to taper the benefit in steps across the five years — 30%, then 20%, then 10% — was legislated and then withdrawn before it took full effect. It was replaced by the flat 27%. If you read about a tapering schedule, you are reading about a rule that was reversed.
  • The maximum duration is five years, and has been since 2019. Any period you previously spent living or working in the Netherlands is generally deducted from it.
  • Rulings that started before 1 January 2024 fall under transitional arrangements and keep their original terms, including the income standard that applied to them.

There is one more change with an immediate deadline attached. The partial non-resident taxpayer status — which let holders of the ruling be treated as non-resident for Dutch tax on certain investment and substantial-shareholding income — was abolished from 1 January 2025. A transitional rule lets people who were already using the ruling in 2023 keep it, but only through to the end of 2026. If that describes you, 2026 is the last year it applies, and it is worth a conversation with an adviser before the year closes rather than after.

Your employer applies, not you

The application is a joint request from you and your employer to the tax authorities, and your employer drives it. The timing matters more than almost anything else in this article: filed within four months of your first working day, the ruling applies retroactively from day one. Filed later, it starts only from the month after submission, and those first months are simply gone. There is no appeal that recovers them.

So confirm explicitly during onboarding that it has been filed, rather than assuming HR has it handled. “Has the 30% application been submitted, and on what date?” is a completely normal question, and in a culture this direct nobody will find it rude. Ask for a copy of the decision when it arrives — you will need it if you change jobs.

What it is worth, roughly

Illustration only

On a gross salary of €60,000 with the ruling at 30%, €18,000 is paid free of payroll tax and €42,000 is taxed as normal income.

Your actual gain is the tax you would otherwise have paid on that €18,000, which depends on your bracket and your personal circumstances. At 27% the same salary gives €16,200 untaxed instead — a reduction of €1,800 in the untaxed portion, not in your pay.

These are illustrative figures to show the shape of it, not a calculation of your position. Thresholds, percentages and brackets all change.

One practical benefit that is easy to overlook: holders of the ruling can usually exchange a foreign driving licence for a Dutch one without retaking the test. For some nationalities that alone is worth several hundred euros and several months.

What can take it away

  • Changing jobs. The ruling does not transfer automatically. A new joint application has to be filed, within a limited window after the previous employment ends, and the new employer has to be willing. Ask about it during the new offer conversation, not after you resign.
  • A gap between jobs. Too long between the end of one contract and the start of the next and the continuity is broken.
  • Falling below the threshold. Reduced hours, extended unpaid leave, or a year in which your taxable salary dips under the norm can end it, not merely pause it.
  • Turning 30 on the reduced threshold, if your salary does not clear the general one.
  • Time already spent here. Previous periods in the Netherlands are deducted from the five years, and some people discover a shorter term than they expected.

Questions worth asking before you sign

  • Is the offer quoted including or excluding the 30% allowance? This single ambiguity accounts for most of the unpleasant surprises.
  • Does the contract guarantee the gross salary if the ruling is refused, or does your pay drop?
  • Who bears the cost if the application is filed late?
  • What happens to the arrangement if the rules change again mid-term?
  • Is my pension accrued on the full salary or the reduced taxable salary?
  • If my ruling starts in 2027, has the offer been calculated at 27% rather than 30%?

That last one is worth raising directly if you are negotiating now. When your ruling starts changes what it is worth, and a package modelled on the old percentage is a package modelled on a rule that will not apply.

This is genuinely not something to guess your way through. The exact thresholds and percentages are revised often, so treat what is written here as the shape of it and confirm the current figures for your situation with the official government guidance, the Dutch tax authorities’ own pages, or I amsterdam’s explainer, before you factor it into a decision. For anything with real money attached, a Dutch tax adviser is worth the fee.
