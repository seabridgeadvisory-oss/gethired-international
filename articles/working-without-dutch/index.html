Why Dutch isn’t always required

Can you get a job in the Netherlands without speaking Dutch?

Yes. The useful answer is where that stops being true, and what the person hiring is really weighing up.

The short answer is yes, and plenty of people are doing it right now. But “yes” on its own is the answer a job board gives you, and it is not much use when you are three months into a search and wondering whether the language is the reason nothing is landing. The honest version has edges to it.

Where English is genuinely enough

There are whole parts of this economy that run in English, and not as a courtesy. If the team is half international, English is simply the working language, and your Dutch is irrelevant to whether you can do the job.

  • Technology and engineering. Software, data, hardware, semiconductors. Teams are assembled from wherever the skills are, and the documentation was in English before you arrived.
  • International corporates with a Dutch base. If the company reports to a global head office, the internal language usually follows.
  • Logistics, supply chain and trade. The Netherlands moves other countries’ goods for a living; the counterparties are foreign by definition.
  • Finance, shared service centres and consultancy serving international clients.
  • Research and academia, where publication and collaboration are in English.
  • Scale-ups, particularly around Amsterdam, Utrecht, Eindhoven and Rotterdam, which hire for speed and for scarce skills.

Where Dutch quietly decides it

Elsewhere the language is not a preference printed in the advert. It is the job.

  • Anything client-facing in a Dutch-speaking market. Sales, account management, customer support. You cannot build trust in a language your customer is translating in their head.
  • Public sector, healthcare, education, law. Regulated, local, and conducted in Dutch.
  • Smaller companies. A team of twelve who all speak Dutch will not switch to English for one person, however much they like you. That is not prejudice, it is arithmetic.
  • Management roles in Dutch-speaking teams, where the job is largely conversation.

Notice that this split is not about seniority or how good you are. It is about who you would be talking to all day.

The question behind the question

Here is the part I only understood properly from sitting on the hiring side.

When a hiring manager hesitates over language, they are usually not asking “can this person do the work?” They have already decided you can, or you would not be in front of them. They are asking something less comfortable and rarely said out loud: what happens to the room when you are in it? Does the team meeting switch to English, and how do the two people who find that effortful feel about it? Will you be able to follow the corridor conversation where the real decision gets made?

That question is answerable, and it is answerable in your favour. But you have to answer it on purpose, because nobody will ask it directly.

What answering it looks like

Weak: “I don’t speak Dutch yet, but I’m planning to learn.” Everyone says this, and most do not.

Stronger: “I’m at A2 and in a class twice a week. I can follow a stand-up in Dutch, I just answer in English. I’d expect to be running my own one-to-ones in Dutch within the year.”

The second version is evidence rather than intention, and it tells them exactly what the room looks like in six months.

What to do while you are still learning

  • State a level, not a feeling. “Dutch: A2, actively studying” is respected. “Basic Dutch” is ambiguous, and ambiguity gets read pessimistically by someone with a stack of applications.
  • Never overstate it. If you claim conversational Dutch, somebody will open the interview in Dutch. That is a bad thirty seconds you never recover from.
  • Show the trajectory. A date, a course, a level you are working towards. Direction beats a current level.
  • Learn the vocabulary of your own field first. Being able to follow a technical conversation in your discipline is worth more than general small talk, and it comes faster.

How to find the roles that really are open to you

The language of the vacancy is the single most reliable filter you have, and it costs nothing to apply. A posting written in English, by a company whose careers page is in English, for a team whose people post in English on LinkedIn, is telling you three times.

Two warnings. First, treat “English-speaking jobs” aggregators with care: many are recycled listings, and some roles tagged English-friendly turn out to need Dutch by the second round. Check the company directly. Second, an advert that says “Dutch is a plus” is worth a conversation; one that says “Dutch and English required” is usually telling you the truth.

And read the team, not just the company. A multinational with an English-language careers site can still have a Dutch-speaking finance department.

The honest summary

Not speaking Dutch does not close the Dutch job market. It narrows it, unevenly, and the narrowing is concentrated in sectors and company sizes you can identify in advance rather than discover after forty rejections. Aim where the door is already open, be precise and truthful about your level, and answer the question nobody asks you.
